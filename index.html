Under FPTP, a plurality of votes can become a majority of seats. The larger question is whether that parliamentary majority becomes the only national power that matters.

First, drop countries with fewer than 10 million people. That removes 116; 82 remain.

Next, drop the 35 countries Freedom House rates Not Free. That leaves 47 countries rated Free or Partly Free for comparison.

Now run the 47 through three institutional arrangements that can disperse power. Once one appears, set that country aside; keep only those without any of the three.

Start inside parliament. 32 elect all or some lower-house seats through proportional rules. Set them aside. 15 still have no proportional component.

Take Peru. Its 130 deputies are elected proportionally across 27 districts. Voters choose a party list and may name preferred candidates; seats are divided among lists rather than handed to one district winner.

Back across the comparison: of the 15 countries left, 9 directly elect a president. The office's power varies, but its mandate comes from voters, not parliament. 6 remain.

Ghana shows what that separation means. Its MPs are elected one per constituency by FPTP. Voters separately choose the executive president nationwide; a winner needs more than 50%, or the top two face a runoff.

Six countries remain without proportional lower-house seats or a directly elected president. 5 have a second chamber, where legislation can be revised, delayed or stopped. 1 remains.

Take Australia. Every state elects 12 senators regardless of population; each mainland territory elects two. All bills need both houses, and the Senate can amend or reject most legislation—and refuse any bill.

Across the full comparison, 46 have at least one of these arrangements. The last is Bangladesh: its general seats use FPTP, its president is not separately elected and it has no second chamber. Zero of three.

Opinion

Bangladesh, the world's most unchecked majoritarian democracy, needed proportional representation. It got the wrong kind.

By Syed Ahlan Jadid and Nazmul Ahasan

Bangladesh’s proposed PR-based upper house is meant to stop an FPTP landslide from making one party all-powerful. But the ballot, allocation formula and constitutional voting rule will decide whether it checks the majority or reproduces it.

Bangladesh’s Constitution is among the world’s easiest to amend: a two-thirds majority in Parliament is enough. In the 1990s, that threshold appeared to work as a safeguard. No party controlled two-thirds, and both constitutional amendments passed during the decade emerged from political consensus.

The arrangement weakened once first-past-the-post began turning modest vote advantages into landslide seat shares. In 2001, BNP and Awami League each won around 40 per cent of the popular vote, yet BNP took 193 seats against Awami League’s 62. BNP then used its supermajority to pass the 14th Amendment, helping set off the political crisis that ended in democratic breakdown.

Awami League repeated the pattern after 2008. BNP retained roughly 30 per cent of the vote but only about 10 per cent of the seats. The resulting two-thirds majority allowed Awami League to pass the 15th Amendment without needing opposition support.

FPTP therefore gives winning parties leverage well beyond their popular vote. The July Charter 2025 proposed a PR-based upper house to interrupt that cycle. BNP’s note of dissent offered a different design: compose the upper house from parties’ lower-house seat shares, carrying the FPTP result upstairs.

The Charter says a party may implement a position recorded in a note of dissent if it puts that position in its manifesto and wins a mandate. But question one of the July 2026 referendum asked voters directly about a PR-based upper house, and more than 70 per cent voted yes. The result leaves competing claims: BNP points to its electoral mandate, while the opposition points to the referendum’s direct mandate.

A technical choice could reshape political alliances

Creating a PR-based upper house is not enough. The supplied analysis tests eight formulas for converting votes into seats. Hare quota is the most proportional of the valid outcomes retained here; D’Hondt is the least. The choice creates different incentives for the largest parties and for their smaller allies and rivals.

For the simulation, we divided Bangladesh into eight divisional districts and allocated the 100 upper-house seats among them by registered voters. We then removed votes cast for independent and rebel candidates from the party-only pool and applied each formula within every division. Those exclusions are a modelling choice, not an unavoidable feature of proportional representation.

The first decision changes the balance before any formula is applied. Independent and rebel candidates received 4,339,706 votes — 5.79 per cent of all valid votes and the third-largest vote bloc. Removing them raises BNP’s share from 49.97 per cent of all valid votes to 53.04 per cent of the party-only pool.

The formula then changes who gets represented. Hare quota gives seats to seven parties, with BNP on 54 and Jamaat on 35. D’Hondt gives BNP 61 and Jamaat 36, while only five parties remain. Islamic Front and Khelafat Majlis disappear; NCP, IAB and BKM are each reduced.

That creates a possible bargaining equilibrium. Hare is more representative but less advantageous to the two largest parties. D’Hondt benefits BNP most and could also appeal to Jamaat by weakening smaller Islamist rivals and reducing NCP’s representation. Negotiations over the formula may therefore separate large parties from smaller allies while favouring the ruling party.

The incentives may extend beyond the initial bargaining. A proportional contest can make running alone more viable for smaller parties, reducing pressure to consolidate against a dominant party. The experiences of Japan’s LDP and Turkey’s AKP are not mechanically identical to Bangladesh’s proposal, but they illustrate the risk the authors identify: durable small parties can coexist with a fragmented opposition and long periods of dominant-party rule.

A separate ballot is the first deterrent

One way to reduce these distortions is to give each voter two ballots: one constituency vote under FPTP for the lower house, and one party-list vote for the upper house. The second ballot would measure the voter’s party preference directly instead of inferring it from a different contest.

That would prevent independent-candidate votes from simply disappearing from the upper-house denominator. The 4.34 million voters who backed independent and rebel candidates in 2026 could still choose a party for the upper chamber, rather than having their lower-house choice excluded from the calculation.

It would also clarify party strategy. If one lower-house ballot determined representation in both chambers, parties could nominate candidates partly to accumulate upper-house votes even where they could not win the constituency. That could fragment the lower-house vote — the vote that decides who forms the government.

The upper house should have a say in constitutional amendments

The July Charter proposes that a constitutional amendment should also pass by a simple majority in the upper house. BNP argues that the upper house should have no role in constitutional amendments. We think both positions are wrong.

The lower house has 350 members and the proposed upper house 100. A constitutional amendment currently needs 234 votes below; a simple-majority rule would require only 51 votes above. That would give a much smaller chamber equal gatekeeping power at a far lower numerical threshold. Requiring two-thirds in both houses — 234 votes in the lower house and 67 in the upper — would make constitutional change depend on broader political agreement.

BNP’s objection is that upper-house members would not be directly elected. But Article 70 prevents MPs from voting against their party on motions covered by party discipline. The July Charter would relax the article in some areas while retaining it for constitutional amendments. On that question, even directly elected lower-house MPs would still be voting as party blocs rather than as autonomous representatives.

A separately elected PR upper house could also reveal support for parties more directly. A voter may choose a constituency candidate for personal competence while disliking that candidate’s party; after the election, Article 70 transfers much of the member’s voting autonomy back to the party. A separate party-list ballot would make the upper house a clearer measure of the support each party actually commands.

Adopting a PR-based upper house is not enough by itself to protect the Constitution or represent voters fairly. The ballot, district design, allocation formula and amendment threshold will determine whether the new chamber becomes a genuine check or another route to concentrated power. Those rules require more public debate before the system is fixed.

Methodology

Upper-house simulation

This is a mechanical counterfactual, not a forecast. It reuses lower-house constituency votes as though they were upper-house list preferences; a separate ballot could produce different choices. Excluding independent candidates is a design decision in this model, not a requirement of every proportional system.

The scrolly shows Hare and D’Hondt, the least and most disproportional of the four valid retained outcomes by the Gallagher index. Droop quota, Hagenbach-Bischoff quota and Webster/Sainte-Laguë are identical in every division-party cell in this dataset. The Imperiali quota and modified Imperiali quota outputs are excluded because the supplied implementation over-allocates the chamber to 101 and 105 seats.

Division magnitudes are apportioned from registered voters with the Hare largest-remainder method. Party seats are then allocated within each division with no party-vote eligibility threshold, levelling tier, candidate-list rule or specified tie-break. The source file should be checked against the authoritative election record before publication.

Opening country comparison

Electoral system classifications follow the International IDEA taxonomy. A country is counted as having proportional seats if its lower house uses List PR, MMP, a parallel system or STV; a separate national mandate if its head of state is directly elected by any method; and a second chamber if its legislature has an upper house. Second chambers vary widely: some represent territories, while others primarily scrutinise and revise legislation.

The country examples use official institutional sources: Peru’s 2026 electoral plan and bicameral guide, the Electoral Commission of Ghana, and the Parliament of Australia.

Population and Freedom House ratings in the underlying file date from 2009 and should be refreshed before publication. The outlier finding is specific to this 47-country comparison, not every country in the world: Papua New Guinea and several microstates fall below the population threshold and also lack all three arrangements.

Bangladesh’s lower house is not purely first-past-the-post. Fifty seats are reserved for women and allocated in proportion to party strength — a partial corrective inside the chamber, but one that cannot alter which party controls it.
