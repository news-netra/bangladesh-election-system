47 In the comparison

Under FPTP, a plurality of votes can become a majority of seats. The larger question is whether that parliamentary majority becomes the only national power that matters.

To compare like with like, keep countries with at least 10 million people rated Free or Partly Free by Freedom House. 47 remain.

Now run the 47 through three institutional arrangements that can disperse power. Once one appears, set that country aside; keep only those without any of the three.

Start inside parliament. 32 elect all or some lower-house seats through proportional rules. 15 still have no PR component.

Of those 15, 9 directly elect a president. The office's power varies, but its mandate comes from voters, not parliament. 6 remain.

Of those six, 5 have a second chamber. Its power may range from review and delay to veto. 1 remains.

46 have at least one of these arrangements. The last is Bangladesh: its general seats use FPTP, its president is not separately elected and it has no second chamber. Zero of three.

The table restores the overlaps: a country may have more than one safeguard. In this 47-country comparison, Bangladesh alone has three NOs.

Opinion

Bangladesh, the world's most unchecked majoritarian democracy, needed proportional representation. It got the wrong kind.

By Syed Ahlan Jadid and Nazmul Ahasan

Bangladesh is debating a second chamber elected by proportional representation. It could become a counterweight to the lower-house majority — or a copy of it. The ballot, district, formula and decision rule decide which.

The reform debate contains two distinct models: use each party’s lower-house vote share to compose the upper house, or use the share of seats it won below. The data supplied for this analysis can simulate only the first; it does not contain the party-by-party lower-house seat totals needed to test the second.

The majority appears before the formula

The supplied simulation starts with votes cast for the lower house, then removes votes for independent and rebel candidates because the upper-house contest is modelled only among party lists.

In the supplied 2026 division totals, the columns coded as independent and rebel candidates combine to 4,339,706 valid votes — more than any party except BNP and Jamaat. Remove that 5.79 per cent and recompute the shares: BNP moves from just below half of all valid votes to a majority of the votes admitted to the model.

“PR” does not name one result

The same party votes, the same eight divisions and the same 100 seats produce different chambers under different rules. Hare quota gives BNP 54 seats and seats seven parties. D’Hondt gives BNP 61 seats and seats five.

If a decision requires 51 of all 100 members, every retained allocation below gives BNP enough seats to pass it alone. A two-thirds requirement of 67 would not. The formula decides who gets represented; the constitutional voting requirement decides whether those representatives can restrain the largest party.

Geography is another electoral rule

The model first assigns the 100 seats to divisions by registered voters: six to Barishal, seven to Sylhet, 24 to Dhaka and between eight and 20 elsewhere. Party seats are then calculated inside each division, not nationally. Those small district magnitudes make it harder for smaller parties to win a seat.

The district design changes the chamber before the formula does. In one national district, Hare would seat 10 parties and give BNP 53 seats; divided eight ways, it seats seven parties and gives BNP 54. National D’Hondt gives BNP 56 seats; division-by-division D’Hondt gives it 61.

Under Hare, BNP has exactly half of Barishal’s seats and shares the largest bloc in Rangpur. D’Hondt turns both into BNP majorities. Khulna remains a Jamaat majority under either method.

Build a counterweight, not a mirror

The weakness is not proportional representation itself. It is reusing the lower-house ballot, treating millions of candidate votes as missing, locking proportionality inside small divisions, and then setting a decision requirement the largest party already clears.

A stronger design

Three choices would measure preferences more directly and require broader agreement

01
Give voters a separate upper-house ballot

Let voters choose a party list for this chamber directly. Lower-house votes for individual candidates would no longer disappear from an unrelated denominator.

02
Combine division lists with national levelling seats

Keep a geographic link, then use a compensatory national tier to restore overall proportionality. Publish the formula, party-eligibility threshold and tie rule before voting.

03
Require two-thirds for constitutional change in both houses

The largest party has 54–61 of 100 seats in these displayed simulations. A two-thirds rule would require agreement beyond it.

The choice is not simply PR or no PR. It is which votes count, how large the districts are, how votes become seats — and what the new chamber is empowered to stop.

Notes on the simulation

This is a mechanical counterfactual, not a forecast. It reuses lower-house constituency votes as though they were upper-house list preferences; a separate ballot could produce different choices. Excluding independent candidates is a design decision in this model, not a requirement of every proportional system.

Only four unique outcomes that return exactly 100 seats are shown. Droop quota, Hagenbach-Bischoff quota and Webster/Sainte-Laguë are identical in every division-party cell in this dataset, so they appear as one result. The Imperiali quota and modified Imperiali quota outputs are excluded because they over-allocate the chamber to 101 and 105 seats.

Division magnitudes are apportioned from registered voters with the Hare largest-remainder method. Party seats are then allocated within each division with no party-vote eligibility threshold, levelling tier, candidate-list rule or specified tie-break. The source file should be checked against the authoritative election record before publication.

Notes on the opening comparison

Electoral system classifications follow the International IDEA taxonomy. A country is counted as having proportional seats if its lower house uses List PR, MMP, a parallel system or STV; a separate national mandate if its head of state is directly elected by any method; and a second chamber if its legislature has an upper house. Second chambers vary widely: some represent territories, while others primarily scrutinise and revise legislation.

Population and Freedom House ratings in the underlying file date from 2009 and should be refreshed before publication. The outlier finding is specific to this 47-country comparison, not every country in the world: Papua New Guinea and several microstates fall below the population threshold and also lack all three arrangements.

Bangladesh’s lower house is not purely first-past-the-post. Fifty seats are reserved for women and allocated in proportion to party strength — a partial corrective inside the chamber, but one that cannot alter which party controls it.
