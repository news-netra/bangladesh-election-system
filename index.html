First-past-the-post can turn less than half the vote into a majority of seats. The question is what else can restrain that majority. Start with 198 political systems.

Fewer than 10 million people, and representation works differently. 82 remain.

Where elections don't decide power, the method is beside the point. 47 remain — and one of them is Bangladesh.

46 of the 47 have at least one arrangement that can keep a simple parliamentary majority from becoming the only source of power.

32 elect all or some parliamentary seats proportionally, bringing vote share closer to seat share.

30 give voters a separate national choice. In some countries that president holds executive power; elsewhere the office still carries a mandate outside parliament.

32 have another legislative chamber. Some represent territories or regions; others revise laws. Their powers range from delay and renewed debate to an outright veto.

Bangladesh has an FPTP elected chamber, no separately elected president and no second chamber. On these measures, a plurality can take the whole elected legislature.

Across the 47-country table, Bangladesh is the only coral row. Its three NO marks show that none of the safeguards is present.

The July Charter commits Bangladesh to a second chamber elected by proportional representation. The sequence above is the argument for why that matters. What follows is the argument over how it should be built — and the finding that the method chosen will decide who it represents.

TK — Why the method is the whole argument

[Reporting TK. There are at least eight established methods for converting votes into seats, and they do not agree. Hare quota produces the most proportional result; Imperiali and D'Hondt favour the largest parties. This is not a technicality — it is the difference between a chamber that represents seven parties and one that represents five.]

TK — What it means for representation

[Reporting TK. BNP holds a majority in every division except Khulna under proportional allocation, but is comparatively underrepresented in the northwest under the current lower house. Independents placed third by vote share nationally and are excluded from PR allocation entirely — a real limitation of composing an upper house from lower-house vote counts.]

Notes on method

Electoral system classifications follow the International IDEA taxonomy. A country is counted as having proportional seats if its lower house uses List PR, MMP, a parallel system or STV; a separate national mandate if its head of state is directly elected by any method; and a second chamber if its legislature has an upper house. Second chambers vary widely: some represent territories, while others primarily scrutinise and revise legislation.

Population and Freedom House ratings in the underlying file date from 2009 and should be refreshed before publication. The outlier finding is specific to this 47-country comparison, not every country in the world: Papua New Guinea and several microstates fall below the population threshold and also lack all three arrangements.

Note that Bangladesh's lower house is not purely first-past-the-post. Fifty seats are reserved for women and allocated in proportion to party strength — a partial corrective inside the chamber, but one that cannot alter which party controls it.
